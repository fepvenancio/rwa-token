Overview / Governance

Who can do what

Every power in the system is a named role on a named contract. This page lists who holds each one once the deployment is finished, what it allows and what it does not, how long it waits, and what still works while something is paused.

In one minute

A role is a permission an account holds on one contract (OpenZeppelin AccessControl). A timelock is a contract that runs a call only after the call has been public for a fixed delay, so anyone can see a change coming and react. After deployment the deployer holds nothing. The main timelock administers every contract, so every change of policy, code or roles waits at least seven days. Day-to-day work, such as issuing, enforcing, settling and fulfilling, belongs to operational accounts and takes effect at once. The guardian, a multisig, can stop things at once but cannot start anything. A second timelock, the recovery timelock, can undo what the guardian did and nothing else.

Authority map

Two kinds of power. Above the line, everything goes through a timelock: governance proposes, waits out the delay, then executes, and the target contract sees the timelock as the caller. Below the line, each account acts directly and at once, but only within its narrow job.

Who holds what after deployment

These are the holders that the two Ignition stages set up, Components then Activate. The contracts do not forbid other holders: whoever holds DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE on a contract can grant and revoke every other role on it, because no contract here changes OpenZeppelin's default role admin. After the hand-over that is the main timelock, so any new holder waits for its delay.

Until Activate finishes, the deployer account administers the tokens, both executors, the credential registry and both timelocks, and owns ProxyDeployer and Fund. The hand-over check assertHandedOver reads every *_ROLE() getter of the timelocks, the registries, the tokens and the executors, and fails if the deployer still holds any of those roles. It also pins the recovery timelock's whole role set, so a deployment that gives it more fails the check. Deployment and governance has the order of the hand-over.

The word "operator" means three things in this code. OPERATOR_ROLE on an executor is the key that submits signed intents. ctx.operator in a movement's context is whoever called the token: the holder, a spender, the agent, the minter or an executor. setOperator on the 7540 vault approves a full delegate of a controller, which can open requests with the owner's assets or shares and choose the receiver of a claim.

The guardian: stop-only

The guardian is a multisig with four powers, all of them ways to stop something, all with no delay:

  1. Pause a token. pause() on a token stops every balance movement except FORCED ones. Each token has its own pause.
  2. Pause an executor. pause() on IntentExecutor or DvPExecutor stops its operator from submitting.
  3. Cancel a timelock operation. With CANCELLER_ROLE it can cancel any operation on the main timelock that has not been executed, including one whose delay has passed.
  4. Block an address or kill an attester. blockSubject stops an address from sending or receiving under CredentialRule. Only an enforcement move can take tokens out of it (a forced transfer, or a burn by the burner); nothing can deliver tokens to it, not even a forced transfer. killAttester turns every credential that attester wrote into exit-only: the holder can leave but not receive.

It cannot schedule or execute timelock operations, unpause, change a hook or a rule list, grant roles or upgrade. On the credential registry it cannot unblock, revive, add an attester or write a credential. It holds nothing on the recovery timelock. A guardian key that is stolen can therefore halt the system, not take value or change policy.

The two timelocks and the recovery path

The main timelock's cancel does not look at what an operation does. So the guardian could cancel a scheduled unpause, or its own removal, again and again. The recovery timelock is the way around it. Governance proposes and executes there, it has the same seven-day delay, and nobody holds CANCELLER_ROLE on it, so what it schedules runs.

RECOVERY_ROLE allows those calls and nothing else. It is deliberately not a role admin, because a role admin could also grant. Instead the tokens and the executors accept it in unpause() next to DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE; their revokeRole override, and the credential registry's, let it revoke GUARDIAN_ROLE; the main timelock's lets it revoke CANCELLER_ROLE; and the credential registry also accepts it in clearGuardianBlock and reviveAttester. A test schedules forbidden calls through it, such as granting a role, swapping a hook, upgrading or proposing on the main timelock, and each one fails with AccessControlUnauthorizedAccount.

Each unpause in a recovery batch must target a contract that is paused when the batch runs, or ExpectedPause reverts the whole batch. Until the batch runs, the guardian keeps all its powers. The recovery path bounds the guardian, not governance: a compromised governance multisig proposes on both timelocks and is out of scope for this control.

The delay

A timelock operation can run no sooner than the timelock's getMinDelay() after it is scheduled (TimelockInsufficientDelay refuses a shorter one). The proposer may choose a longer delay, and a later change of the minimum does not move operations already scheduled. Components gives both timelocks the same minDelay parameter, seven days by default. The module does not refuse a smaller value; the hand-over check does, by asserting getMinDelay() >= 7 days on both. Changing a delay is itself one of that timelock's operations, because updateDelay accepts only the timelock as caller.

The registries, the hook, the rules, the fund and ProxyDeployer have no pause. In the credential registry the guardian's equivalent is blocking an address or killing an attester. The incident runbook puts the pause, the agent's enforcement and the resume in order.

Why it is built this way

  • Stopping is fast, starting is slow. The guardian can only stop, so the one power with no delay cannot move value or change policy. Everything that starts something again, or changes it, waits for a timelock and is public while it waits.
  • A second timelock bounds the first one's cancellers. Cancelling on the main timelock is blind to what is cancelled, so the guardian could block its own removal. The recovery timelock has no cancellers and only the narrow powers to undo a guardian, so governance always has a path the guardian cannot block.
  • Rules and the hook have no roles of their own. They ask the token at call time who its admin or agent is. One shared rule instance can serve every token, and moving a token's admin role moves who configures its policy, with nothing else to update. The hand-over check asserts that no rule exposes hasRole or owner.
  • Only the timelock can upgrade. UPGRADER_ROLE goes to the main timelock alone, and an upgrade must target reviewed code of the same template.
  • Executors are contracts, not keys. EXECUTOR_ROLE on IntentToken is held by the two executor contracts only, so a signed intent always runs through code that the guardian can pause and that checks what it submits.
  • No expiring pause. Nothing in the code ends a pause by time. Only a timelock lifts it, so a security control does not turn into a timer.
